SaaS Growth Metrics That Actually Matter: 10 KPIs to Track

Learn how to measure SaaS growth with the revenue, retention, acquisition, activation, and efficiency metrics that reveal whether recurring growth can last.

GrowthForge•Published September 20, 2026•Updated September 20, 2026•Growth Strategy

SaaS growth metrics measure how effectively a subscription business acquires customers, creates product value, grows recurring revenue, retains accounts, expands revenue, and recovers its growth investment. The core SaaS growth KPIs are MRR or ARR growth, net and gross revenue retention, churn, CAC, CAC payback, lifetime value, conversion, activation, and expansion revenue.

Used together, these metrics for SaaS growth show more than a top-line revenue number. They reveal whether growth is coming from qualified acquisition, successful onboarding, durable retention, customer expansion, or increasingly efficient go-to-market execution.

What are SaaS growth metrics?

SaaS growth metrics are the measurements used to evaluate the health and repeatability of a software subscription business. They connect the full customer lifecycle: acquisition, conversion, activation, recurring revenue, retention, expansion, and efficiency.

They differ from general SaaS KPIs because they focus on the drivers and quality of growth. A general operating KPI may report activity, such as website sessions, total signups, or support tickets. A growth metric connects activity to customer value, recurring revenue, or the cost required to produce that revenue.

They also differ from vanity metrics. Large signup totals may look positive, but they do not explain whether users activate, convert, renew, or expand. Financial metrics such as total revenue and cash balance remain essential, but SaaS revenue metrics explain the customer behavior underneath those outcomes.

SaaS revenue metrics: MRR, ARR, and growth rate

MRR growth rate

Monthly recurring revenue (MRR) is the normalized monthly revenue from active recurring subscriptions. MRR growth rate measures how quickly that recurring revenue changes from one period to the next.

Formula: (Ending MRR − Beginning MRR) ÷ Beginning MRR × 100

Use beginning and ending MRR from comparable periods. Then review the movement behind the number: new MRR, expansion MRR, contraction MRR, and churned MRR. A rising MRR growth rate is more useful when retention and acquisition efficiency remain visible beside it. See GrowthForge guidance on MRR, ARR, and revenue growth planning for the reporting context behind these SaaS revenue metrics.

ARR growth

Annual recurring revenue (ARR) annualizes recurring subscription revenue, commonly using current MRR multiplied by 12 for businesses with monthly subscriptions.

Formula: ARR = MRR × 12

ARR growth rate: (Ending ARR − Beginning ARR) ÷ Beginning ARR × 100

ARR is useful for annual planning and communicating recurring-revenue scale. It should not replace the monthly MRR bridge, which shows whether growth came from new customers, expansion, contraction, or churn.

Expansion revenue

Expansion revenue is additional recurring revenue from existing customers through upgrades, more seats, higher usage, add-ons, or cross-sells.

Formula: Expansion MRR = MRR added from existing customers during the period

Expansion is a key input to NRR. Analyze it by customer segment, plan, product usage pattern, and acquisition channel to understand which customers find growing value over time. A useful next step is to analyze net revenue retention and customer expansion alongside product adoption.

Retention and churn metrics for SaaS growth

Customer churn rate

Customer churn measures the share of customers that cancel or fail to renew during a period.

Formula: Customers lost during period ÷ Customers at start of period × 100

Customer churn shows account loss, but it does not show the value of those accounts. Compare it with revenue churn, cohort retention, and segment-level behavior. Different customer segments can have materially different retention patterns.

Revenue churn rate

Revenue churn measures the recurring revenue lost from cancellations and, depending on the definition used, contractions during a period.

Formula: Churned MRR during period ÷ Beginning MRR × 100

Document whether contraction revenue is included in your definition. Revenue churn can reveal risk that customer-count churn hides, especially when high-value accounts leave or downgrade.

Gross revenue retention (GRR)

GRR measures how much recurring revenue from the starting customer base remains after churn and contraction, before adding expansion.

Formula: (Beginning MRR − Churned MRR − Contraction MRR) ÷ Beginning MRR × 100

GRR isolates baseline retention health. Because expansion is excluded, it helps teams see whether upgrades are masking preventable losses. Use it to focus retention work on onboarding, support, product value, pricing fit, and renewal risk.

Net revenue retention (NRR)

NRR measures how recurring revenue from the starting customer base changes after churn, contraction, and expansion.

Formula: (Beginning MRR − Churned MRR − Contraction MRR + Expansion MRR) ÷ Beginning MRR × 100

NRR indicates whether existing customers collectively generate more or less revenue over time. It is most actionable when paired with GRR: GRR identifies underlying losses, while NRR adds the impact of expansion. Build retention actions around cohorts and lifecycle signals to improve customer retention rather than relying only on an aggregate renewal report.

Acquisition and conversion metrics

Conversion rate

Conversion rate measures the percentage of people or accounts that complete a defined next step, such as visitor-to-lead, lead-to-demo, trial-to-paid, or opportunity-to-customer.

Formula: Number completing the target action ÷ Number eligible to complete it × 100

The formula is simple, but the denominator must be consistent. Define the source population, time window, qualification criteria, and conversion event before comparing periods or channels. Funnel conversion helps reveal whether the constraint is demand quality, sales process, pricing, or onboarding.

Customer acquisition cost (CAC)

CAC is the sales and marketing investment required to acquire one new customer.

Formula: Sales and marketing costs for the period ÷ New customers acquired in the period

For a decision-useful CAC, consistently define which costs are included and align the cost period with the customers acquired. Review CAC by channel, segment, product, and sales motion when the data is reliable enough to support it. Activation rate and product value metrics

Activation rate

Activation rate measures the percentage of new users or accounts that reach a meaningful product value event. That event should represent an action associated with realizing the product's intended benefit, not simply logging in.

Formula: New users or accounts reaching the activation event ÷ Eligible new users or accounts × 100

Activation connects acquisition to retention. If signups rise but activation falls, growth may be attracting less-qualified demand or creating onboarding friction. Define one primary activation event, record the time to reach it, and revisit the definition as the product and customer journey evolve.

Feature adoption and time to value

Feature adoption tracks whether users engage with product capabilities that matter to their job or use case. Time to value tracks how long it takes to reach the activation event. These are leading indicators when they are tied to later conversion, retention, or expansion behavior.

Do not treat every product action as equally valuable. Focus on behaviors that reflect a customer receiving the core outcome your product promises. This is particularly important for product-led teams refining their SaaS pricing, product-led growth, or go-to-market strategy.

Efficiency metrics: CAC payback, LTV, and LTV:CAC

CAC payback period

CAC payback period estimates how long it takes for the gross profit from a newly acquired customer to recover the cost of acquiring that customer.

Formula: CAC ÷ (New customer monthly recurring revenue × Gross margin)

Use the revenue and gross-margin assumptions that best match your reporting model. Payback complements CAC: CAC shows the acquisition cost, while payback adds the speed at which that investment returns. It is particularly useful when comparing channels, segments, contract structures, or sales motions.

Lifetime value (LTV)

LTV estimates the gross profit expected from a customer relationship. It is a model, not a guaranteed outcome, so its inputs and assumptions should be visible.

Simple formula: Average monthly revenue per customer × Gross margin ÷ Monthly revenue churn rate

This simplified approach can be directionally useful, but it becomes unreliable if churn patterns vary significantly by cohort or if the customer base is still small. Cohort analysis and observed retention curves often produce a more useful view of customer value.

LTV:CAC ratio

LTV:CAC compares estimated customer lifetime value with the cost required to acquire that customer.

Formula: LTV ÷ CAC

The ratio is most useful when CAC and LTV are calculated consistently by segment. Review it with CAC payback and retention rather than treating it as a standalone score. A seemingly favorable LTV:CAC calculation can be misleading if its churn or margin assumptions are weak.
