SaaS Growth Metrics That Actually Matter: 15 KPIs to Track

SaaS growth metrics measure how a subscription business acquires, activates, retains, and expands customers while growing recurring revenue efficiently. This guide explains the SaaS growth KPIs that matter, how to calculate them, and how to use them to make better growth decisions.

GrowthForge•Published September 20, 2026•Updated September 20, 2026•Growth Strategy

SaaS growth metrics measure how effectively a subscription business acquires customers, creates product value, grows recurring revenue, retains accounts, expands revenue, and recovers its growth investment. The core SaaS growth KPIs include monthly recurring revenue (MRR), annual recurring revenue (ARR), retention, churn, customer acquisition cost (CAC), customer lifetime value (LTV), conversion, activation, and expansion revenue.

Used together, these metrics to measure SaaS growth show more than a top-line revenue number. They reveal whether growth is coming from qualified acquisition, successful onboarding, durable retention, customer expansion, or increasingly efficient go-to-market execution.

What are SaaS growth metrics?

SaaS growth metrics are the measurements used to evaluate the health and repeatability of a software subscription business. They connect the full customer lifecycle: acquisition, conversion, activation, recurring revenue, retention, expansion, and efficiency.

They differ from general SaaS KPIs because they focus on the drivers and quality of growth. A general operating KPI may report activity, such as website sessions, total signups, or support tickets. A growth metric connects activity to customer value, recurring revenue, or the cost required to produce that revenue.

They also differ from finance metrics and product-usage metrics alone. Total revenue and cash balance remain essential, while feature usage can reveal meaningful behavior. SaaS business metrics become growth metrics when they help explain whether customers reach value, convert, renew, and expand at a sustainable cost.

SaaS revenue metrics: MRR, ARR, growth rate, and ARPA

Monthly recurring revenue (MRR) and MRR growth rate

Monthly recurring revenue (MRR) is the normalized monthly revenue from active recurring subscriptions. MRR growth rate measures how quickly that recurring revenue changes from one period to the next.

Formula: (Ending MRR − Beginning MRR) ÷ Beginning MRR × 100

Beginning MRR is recurring revenue at the start of the period, and ending MRR is recurring revenue at the end. Review the movement behind the result: new MRR, expansion MRR, contraction MRR, and churned MRR. A rising MRR growth rate is more useful when retention and acquisition efficiency remain visible beside it.

Annual recurring revenue (ARR) and ARR growth

Annual recurring revenue (ARR) annualizes recurring subscription revenue. For businesses with monthly subscriptions, it is commonly calculated from current MRR.

Formula: ARR = MRR × 12

ARR growth rate: (Ending ARR − Beginning ARR) ÷ Beginning ARR × 100

ARR is useful for annual planning and communicating recurring-revenue scale. It should not replace the monthly MRR bridge, which explains whether growth came from new customers, expansion, contraction, or churn.

Average revenue per account (ARPA) or user (ARPU)

Average revenue per account (ARPA), sometimes called average revenue per user (ARPU), measures recurring revenue generated by an average active account or user.

Formula: Recurring revenue during the period ÷ Average active accounts or users during the period

Define whether the denominator is accounts, paying customers, or users, then use that definition consistently. ARPA helps explain whether revenue growth is coming from more customers, higher-value customers, or both. Review it with expansion revenue and churn so price, packaging, and customer mix are not interpreted in isolation.

Expansion revenue

Expansion revenue is additional recurring revenue from existing customers through upgrades, more seats, higher usage, add-ons, or cross-sells.

Formula: Expansion MRR = MRR added from existing customers during the period

Expansion is a key input to NRR. Analyze it by customer segment, plan, product usage pattern, and acquisition channel to understand which customers find growing value over time. Review expansion alongside customer retention and expansion strategy insights rather than treating upgrades as separate from the customer experience that enabled them.

Retention, churn, GRR, and NRR metrics

Customer churn rate

Customer churn measures the share of customers that cancel or fail to renew during a period.

Formula: Customers lost during period ÷ Customers at start of period × 100

Customer churn shows account loss, but it does not show the value of those accounts. Compare it with revenue churn, cohort retention, and segment-level behavior. Different customer segments can have materially different retention patterns.

Revenue churn rate

Revenue churn measures the recurring revenue lost from cancellations and, depending on the definition used, contractions during a period.

Formula: Churned MRR during period ÷ Beginning MRR × 100

Document whether contraction revenue is included in your definition. Revenue churn can reveal risk that customer-count churn hides, especially when high-value accounts leave or downgrade.

Gross revenue retention (GRR)

Gross revenue retention (GRR) measures how much recurring revenue from the starting customer base remains after churn and contraction, before adding expansion.

Formula: (Beginning MRR − Churned MRR − Contraction MRR) ÷ Beginning MRR × 100

Beginning MRR represents revenue from accounts at the start of the period. Churned MRR is revenue lost from cancellations, and contraction MRR is revenue lost from downgrades. GRR isolates baseline retention health because expansion is excluded.

Net revenue retention (NRR)

Net revenue retention (NRR) measures how recurring revenue from the starting customer base changes after churn, contraction, and expansion.

Formula: (Beginning MRR − Churned MRR − Contraction MRR + Expansion MRR) ÷ Beginning MRR × 100

NRR indicates whether existing customers collectively generate more or less revenue over time. It is most actionable when paired with GRR: GRR identifies underlying losses, while NRR adds the impact of expansion. Build retention actions around cohorts and lifecycle signals to improve customer retention and reduce churn rather than relying only on an aggregate renewal report.

Acquisition and conversion metrics

Conversion rate

Conversion rate measures the percentage of people or accounts that complete a defined next step, such as visitor-to-lead, lead-to-demo, trial-to-paid, or opportunity-to-customer.

Formula: Number completing the target action ÷ Number eligible to complete it × 100

The denominator must be consistent. Define the source population, time window, qualification criteria, and conversion event before comparing periods or channels. Funnel conversion helps reveal whether the constraint is demand quality, sales process, pricing, or onboarding.

Trial-to-paid conversion rate

Trial-to-paid conversion rate measures the share of eligible trials that become paying customers within a defined conversion window.

Formula: New paying customers from eligible trials ÷ Eligible trials × 100

An eligible trial should be defined consistently, especially when trial lengths or qualification rules vary. Interpret this metric beside activation and time to value. A weak rate may reflect unqualified signups, unclear product value, pricing friction, or an onboarding gap.

Customer acquisition cost (CAC)

Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is the sales and marketing investment required to acquire one new customer.

Formula: Sales and marketing costs for the period ÷ New customers acquired in the period

Sales and marketing costs may include paid media, team compensation, agency fees, software, and other acquisition expenses, depending on the reporting definition. For a decision-useful CAC, define costs consistently and align the cost period with customers acquired. Review CAC by channel, segment, product, and sales motion when the data is reliable enough to support it.
